Fake Cream, Real Victims: Influencer Ads and Consumer Rights in Kurdistan
23 Oct 2026 · @Qamar albahr
A skin cream called "Almas" (کرێمی ئەڵماس), promoted on social media by entertainer Fakhir Hariri and other well-known Kurdish personalities, has been described as fake in a viral video that says thousands of buyers were misled. According to that video, the jars sold for 25,000 to 35,000 Iraqi dinars each.
The case has sparked a wider debate across Kurdish social media. Who is responsible when a product promoted by a famous face turns out to be counterfeit or unsafe: the maker, the seller, or the person who sold it to their followers?
Three videos sit at the center of that debate. The first is a commentary reel accusing the advertisers. The second is a Kurdistan24 news report on a crackdown against whitening medicines that, it says, celebrities had promoted. The third is Fakhir Hariri's own response, titled "A clarification about Almas cream."
The accusation: "Hundreds of them swore by it"
The first video, shared by the page HOKAR, pairs a presenter speaking into a microphone on an Erbil street at night with clips of men holding up jars of the cream. On-screen captions read "What's your opinion?", "Hundreds of them swore by it" and "It was sold for 35 thousand." The post's caption states that the cream Fakhir Hariri advertised turned out to be fake and that thousands of people were deceived by the ad.
The presenter opens with a warning: before trusting an advert, find out whether the product is genuinely safe or counterfeit. He says the cream was promoted by Hariri together with several other artists, some of whom swore on the Quran that it worked. His claims about the product are:
• It sold for between 25,000 and 35,000 dinars.
• It was being produced locally in Erbil, not by an established manufacturer.
• It had no genuine origin, and even its label sticker was fake.
He then turns to the law. He argues that consumer-protection rules in force in the Kurdistan Region and Iraq guarantee that a buyer who purchases a product because of an advert has the right to safety. In his view, when an advertised product proves fake, the advertiser cannot simply say "it had a sticker" and walk away. A buyer who files a court complaint can, he says, have the advertiser treated as a partner of the person who committed the fraud.
He closes with blunt advice: don't trust the models, and don't trust the groups that replace them either.
The official warning: Kurdistan24 on whitening pills
The second video is a Kurdistan24 news reel filmed during an inspection of market stalls, with uniformed security officers present. Its headline caption reads: "Artists and well-known people advertised this medicine." Shelves of cosmetics and supplements are shown, and the speaker holds up a pink bottle of whitening pills.
The speaker, identified in the post as Rasul Abdulkhaliq, makes four points:
• The product is sold as a skin-whitening treatment, and he warns it can cause serious harm, including cancer.
• Medicines like this should only be sold through licensed pharmacies, but these were on open market stalls with no permit to hold medicine.
• The Ministry of Health sticker on the product does not mean it entered the market legally.
• Several artists and public figures promoted it, and people should not trust such ads.
His central message to the public is direct: please do not buy medicine online. The post's caption carries the same warning against being swayed by social-media adverts for whitening and beauty products.
This report concerns whitening pills rather than the Almas cream itself. Its relevance is the pattern it confirms: unlicensed health and beauty products, celebrity promotion, and buyers left exposed.
The response: Fakhir Hariri says the sticker is not his problem
In the third video, posted on his own page under the title "A clarification about Almas cream," Fakhir Hariri speaks directly to the camera. He does not apologize. Instead, he argues that responsibility lies elsewhere.
His main arguments:
• The product carried a Ministry of Health sticker. Whether that sticker is genuine or forged, he says, is not his problem. Checking it is the ministry's job.
• The ministry should pursue the owner. If the product is bad, he says, its owner should be punished, and he offers to tell "Dr. Rasul" who the owner is and where the business is located.
• He is being singled out. He says half of Kurdistan's artists advertised the product, yet criticism is aimed only at him.
• He stands by his conduct. He says he advertised with his head held high, deceived no one, and will not be brought down by the controversy.
His defense rests on one idea: an official-looking label is enough to clear the advertiser. That is precisely the claim the first video and the Kurdistan24 report reject. Both say the existence of a sticker proves nothing about whether a product is safe or legal.
What the law says
Iraq's Consumer Protection Law No. 1 of 2010 does not treat advertisers as bystanders. It defines an advertiser as anyone who promotes a product or service, directly or through others, and applies the law to them alongside makers and sellers.
Key provisions:
Article
What it says
1
Defines "advertiser" as anyone who promotes a product or service, directly or through others
3
Applies the law to everyone who makes, sells, markets, imports or advertises goods and services
6
Gives consumers the right to full product information, proof of purchase, and compensation through the courts
7
Requires suppliers and advertisers to verify product details before advertising, and bars promoting products that don't meet approved standards
9
Bans suppliers and advertisers from fraud, deception or misleading acts, and from selling goods without full ingredients or required warnings
10
Sets penalties for breaking Article 9: at least three months in prison and/or a fine of at least one million dinars
Article 7 matters most here. Under that text, an advertiser's duty begins before the advert: they are expected to check what they promote. That cuts against the argument that a sticker shifts all responsibility to the ministry.
How the federal law is applied in the Kurdistan Region, and whether an influencer post counts as "advertising" in court, has not been tested publicly in this case. That is a question for a lawyer, and a useful one for journalists to put to the region's courts and Ministry of Health.
Why it matters: fame sells trust
The Almas case shows why celebrity ads work so well, and why they can be dangerous. Followers don't buy from an unknown seller; they buy from someone they feel they know. When that person swears on the Quran that a cream works, the oath carries more weight than any label.
That trust creates responsibility. Three gaps stand out across the three videos:
• Verification. Nobody in the videos says they checked where the product was made or tested it before promoting it.
• Disclosure. Viewers are rarely told whether a creator was paid, how much, or by whom.
• Accountability. When problems surface, each party points elsewhere: the influencer to the sticker, the sticker to the ministry, the ministry to the market.
Health and beauty products raise the stakes further. A bad phone charger wastes money. A counterfeit cream or unlicensed whitening pill can damage skin and health, as the Kurdistan24 report warns.
What consumers can do
1. Buy medicines and medicated creams only from licensed pharmacies, not from social-media pages or market stalls.
2. Treat a celebrity endorsement as an advert, not as evidence that a product works or is safe.
3. Don't rely on a sticker alone. Ask the pharmacist or the health directorate whether the product is registered.
4. Keep the receipt, the packaging and screenshots of the advert you saw.
5. If a product harms you or turns out fake, report it to the health authorities and consider a court complaint. The law covers both the seller and the advertiser.
